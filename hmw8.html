📚 Exercise 1: Modes of Convergence in Probability Theory

Four modes of convergence of random variables are defined, compared through counterexamples that are simulated live, and tied to their roles in the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem. A simulation also shows why √n appears: it comes from the additivity of variance under independence.

a.s. ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution L² ⇒ in probability Var(Sₙ) = nσ² (independent) ⇒ Sₙ/√n has constant variance

Implications

a.s. ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution L² ⇒ in probability Lⁿ (q > p) ⇒ Lᵖ (p ≥ 1) Lᵖ (p ≥ 1) ⇒ in probability (Markov)
  • L² ⇒ probability: Chebyshev/Markov, P(|Xn−X|>ε) ≤ E[(Xn−X)²]/ε².
  • a.s. ⇒ probability: P(|Xn−X|>ε) ≤ P(supk≥n|Xk−X|>ε) → 0.
  • probability ⇒ distribution: deviations small in probability cannot move the CDF at its continuity points.

Partial converses

  • Distribution → constant ⇒ probability. If Xn → c in distribution, then Xn → c in probability. This is why LLN statements about X̄n → μ can use any mode.
  • Probability ⇒ a.s. along a subsequence.
  • Borel–Cantelli: if Σn P(|Xn−X|>ε) < ∞ for all ε, then Xn → X a.s.
  • Probability + uniform integrability ⇒ L¹ (Vitali). A.s. + domination ⇒ L¹ (dominated convergence).
  • No other converse holds in general: the next tab gives a counterexample for each missing arrow.

Why √n? Variance of sums of independent vs correlated increments

Increments: independent Xi = Exp(1) − 1 (mean 0, variance 1, skewed), and equicorrelated Gaussian increments with correlation ρ (variance 1). For independent (or just uncorrelated) increments:

Var(Sₙ) = nσ² Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n → 0 Var(Sₙ/√n) = σ² Equicorrelated: Var(Sₙ) = nσ²(1 + (n−1)ρ) ⇒ Var(Sₙ/n) → ρσ² ≠ 0

With independence the variance of Sn adds up, so its standard deviation grows like √n. Dividing by n therefore shrinks the fluctuations (LLN) and dividing by √n keeps them of constant size (CLT). With positive correlation the variance grows like n²ρ: fluctuations are of order n, the sample mean does not converge to μ (it converges to a random variable) and no √n normalisation works.

Var(Sn/√n) vs n

Independent: flat at σ² = 1. Correlated: grows like 1 + (n−1)ρ.

CLT: distribution of Sn/√n (independent case)

√n in statistical inference

  • Standard error: SE(X̄) = σ/√n, because Var(X̄) = nσ²/n² under independence.
  • Cost of precision: halving the error needs 4 times as much data. Confidence-interval width ∝ 1/√n.
  • Asymptotic normality: √n(θ̂n − θ) → N(0, V) (d) for sample means, MLEs and many M-estimators. The factor √n is exactly the rate that makes the limit non-degenerate.
  • Test statistics: z = √n(X̄ − μ0)/σ and its t version have a limit law free of n.

Conclusions

  • The modes are ordered by strength, but each answers a different question: where does Xn go (probability, a.s., L²) versus what law does it follow (distribution).
  • LLN is about convergence to a constant, so the weak mode is enough and distribution convergence is equivalent to it. The strong LLN adds the guarantee on single trajectories.
  • CLT can only be stated in distribution: Sn/√n does not converge to any random variable in probability, a fact the “flip” example mimics.
  • Convergence in L² and in probability can hold without a.s. (typewriter). A.s. can hold without L² (spike): rare huge values matter for moments but not for paths.

Why independence matters

  • Independence (or zero correlation) makes variances add, which gives the √n scale. Any positive correlation inflates the variance and can destroy both LLN and the √n law (see the table above).
  • The CLT additionally needs finite variance (or Lindeberg-type conditions for non-identical summands). Heavy tails, as in the Cauchy law, give no CLT and no LLN.
  • In inference, all standard errors, intervals and p-values built on √n assume this structure: dependent data (time series, clusters) need corrected variances.

📚 Exercise 2: Conditional Probability, Bayes’ Theorem and Statistical Inference

Bayes’ theorem turns prior beliefs and the likelihood of the data into a posterior. The page works through three examples: a diagnostic test (discrete hypotheses), a coin (Beta–Binomial) and a normal mean (Normal–Normal), and discusses advantages, limitations and the influence of the prior.

P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) P(θ|D) = P(D|θ)·P(θ) / P(D) posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

Conditional probability

P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B), P(B) > 0

Restrict the sample space to B and renormalise. Consequences: the product rule P(A∩B) = P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A), the law of total probability P(B) = Σi P(B|Ai)P(Ai) for a partition (Ai), and independence as the special case P(A|B) = P(A).

In statistics the same idea appears as conditional frequencies (rows of a contingency table), regression E[Y|X], and the conditional distribution of the data given a parameter, P(D|θ).

Bayes’ theorem

P(Ai|B) = P(B|Ai)P(Ai) / Σj P(B|Aj)P(Aj)

It follows directly from the product rule and total probability. It reverses the conditioning: from P(data | hypothesis), which a model gives us, to P(hypothesis | data), which is what we want.

The three components of Bayesian inference

Prior π(θ)

What is believed about θ before the data: previous studies, expert knowledge, or a deliberately vague choice. It is a probability distribution on the parameter.

Likelihood L(θ) = P(D|θ)

The probability (density) of the observed data as a function of θ. It is not a distribution over θ: it need not integrate to 1. It carries all the information in the data.

Posterior π(θ|D)

The updated belief: π(θ|D) = L(θ)π(θ) / P(D) with evidence P(D) = ∫ L(θ)π(θ) dθ, a normalising constant. Hence posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.

Odds form and sequential updating

posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio

Yesterday’s posterior is today’s prior. Updating with data in batches or one point at a time gives the same final posterior (for conditionally independent observations).

Why updating is central

  • It gives a probability statement about the parameter itself (e.g. P(θ > 0.5 | data) = 0.93), which is what users usually want. A frequentist confidence interval has no such interpretation.
  • It combines prior knowledge and data in a coherent, mathematically optimal way, and the weight of the data grows with n.
  • It supports sequential learning, prediction (average over the posterior) and decision-making (minimise posterior expected loss).
  • With a flat prior and a lot of data the posterior is concentrated around the MLE: Bayesian and frequentist answers agree numerically (Bernstein–von Mises), while differing in interpretation.

Coin with unknown bias p: Beta prior, Binomial likelihood

Prior p ∼ Beta(a, b). Data: k heads in n flips. Because the Beta family is conjugate to the Binomial likelihood, the posterior is again Beta:

p | data ∼ Beta(a + k, b + n − k) E[p|data] = (a + k)/(a + b + n) = w·(a/(a+b)) + (1 − w)·(k/n), w = (a+b)/(a+b+n)

Influence of the prior as data accumulate

Flip a coin with true p, update three different priors after every flip, and compare the posterior means with the MLE k/n.

A strong prior pulls the estimate toward its own mean when data are scarce (“shrinkage”), and its weight (a+b)/(a+b+n) fades as n grows, so all posteriors approach the data. A prior that gives probability 0 to the truth would never be corrected (Cromwell’s rule).

Mean of a normal with known variance: Normal prior, Normal likelihood

μ ∼ N(μ0, τ²), x̄ | μ ∼ N(μ, σ²/n) ⇒ μ | x̄ ∼ N(m, s²) 1/s² = 1/τ² + n/σ² m = w·x̄ + (1 − w)·μ0, w = (n/σ²) / (1/τ² + n/σ²)

Precisions add, and the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of prior mean and sample mean.

Effect of the sample size (same prior, same x̄)

The weight on the data tends to 1 and the posterior standard deviation shrinks like σ/√n. As τ → ∞ (a flat prior) the 95% credible interval coincides with the classical interval x̄ ± 1.96σ/√n, but the Bayesian one can be read as “P(μ in the interval | data) = 0.95”.

Advantages

  • Direct answers: posterior probabilities of hypotheses and credible intervals with the natural interpretation.
  • Prior information is used rather than ignored: crucial with little data, rare events (diagnostics), or when earlier studies exist.
  • Sequential and coherent updating: new data refine the previous conclusion without restarting.
  • Uncertainty propagates naturally into predictions and decisions (posterior predictive, expected loss).
  • Flexible models: hierarchical models, missing data, and regularisation (a Gaussian prior is ridge regression, a Laplace prior is lasso).
  • Applications: medical testing, spam filters, A/B testing, forecasting, reliability, machine learning.

Limitations

  • Prior choice is subjective and can be contested. With little data different priors give visibly different posteriors (tab 3). Sensitivity analysis is needed.
  • Computation: only conjugate cases have closed forms. Otherwise one needs MCMC, variational inference or numerical integration of P(D).
  • Model misspecification: the posterior is only as good as the likelihood. A wrong model gives confidently wrong answers.
  • Improper or very vague priors can produce improper posteriors or paradoxes, and are not invariant in the same way across reparametrisations (a uniform prior on p is not uniform on log-odds).
  • Zero prior mass is final: a hypothesis with prior 0 stays at 0 whatever the data (Cromwell’s rule: avoid 0 and 1).

Influence on statistical conclusions

  • Small n: the prior matters a lot: in the diagnostic example the prevalence alone can turn a “95% accurate” test into a positive predictive value below 20%.
  • Large n: the likelihood dominates (weight of the prior ≈ (a+b)/n) and Bayesian and frequentist estimates agree.
  • Informative vs weak priors: informative priors reduce variance at the price of bias if they are wrong. Weakly informative priors are a compromise.
  • Reporting: always state the prior, show how conclusions change under alternatives, and distinguish the likelihood (what the data say) from the prior (what we assumed).
